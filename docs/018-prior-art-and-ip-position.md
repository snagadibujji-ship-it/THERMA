# 018 — Prior Art, IP Position, and Invention-Disclosure Boundary

## 1. Purpose
This file records the IP position of GHIA THERMA as a research-stage project. It distinguishes the established thermoacoustic field from the project-specific architectural hypothesis and defines what evidence would be needed before making a novelty or patent claim.

## 2. Project identity
THERMA is a GHIA project. TA-CRX is the internal engineering codename retained in historical records. THERMA is the product-family name used for the broader reversible thermal platform.

## 3. General technical category
The project sits within thermoacoustic engines, thermoacoustic refrigerators, heat-driven thermoacoustic refrigeration, thermoacoustic heat pumps, waste-heat utilization, thermal management, heat exchangers, control, and modular thermal systems.

## 4. Non-novel foundation
THERMA does not claim invention of thermoacoustics itself. Acoustic pressure/velocity oscillation, thermoacoustic engines, refrigerators, heat-driven refrigeration and heat-pump operation are established research areas.

## 5. No blanket novelty claim
The existence of a working project concept, simulation notebook, generated image, or conversation record does not establish that the integrated arrangement is novel or non-obvious over prior art.

## 6. Prior-art categories
The relevant prior-art search must include at least:
- heat-driven thermoacoustic engines;
- thermoacoustic refrigerators;
- multi-stage thermoacoustic systems;
- looped thermoacoustic architectures;
- heat accumulation and buffer systems;
- waste-heat-driven refrigeration;
- reversible thermoacoustic refrigerator/heat-pump systems;
- heat exchanger and regenerator arrangements;
- adaptive acoustic control;
- distributed thermal networks.

## 7. Important existing principle
Published and patented systems demonstrate heat-driven acoustic cooling and acoustic thermal pumping. Therefore the claim scope must focus on a specific combination or implementation rather than the broad physical principle.

## 8. Potential THERMA differentiation
The project hypothesis combines controlled heat-source interfaces, fixed modular thermoacoustic cores, adaptive acoustic/thermal routing, reversible cooling/heating operation, serviceable thermal modules, distributed waste-heat utilization, and explicit safety isolation.

## 9. Potential claimable architecture
A future invention disclosure could test a claim around a specific integrated architecture in which a conditioned thermal source drives a fixed thermoacoustic engine section, an acoustic routing network transfers power to a fixed refrigeration section, and thermal interfaces can be reconfigured for heating operation through a controlled neutral state.

## 10. Potential control claim
A second potential area is a control method that uses measured source temperature, sink temperature, load temperature, pressure, flow and acoustic state to select operating states such as normal, economy, boost, degraded, neutral/transition and safe shutdown.

## 11. Potential thermal-routing claim
Another possible area is the use of a controlled secondary thermal loop with upstream high-temperature protection so that diverse heat sources are accepted without exposing the acoustic core to raw industrial temperature excursions.

## 12. Potential modularity claim
The use of multiple parallel or staged fixed thermoacoustic modules could be part of an architecture claim if the exact arrangement, control coordination and fault-tolerance behavior are shown to be distinct from known systems.

## 13. Potential safety claim
A specific fault-management sequence may be relevant if it combines independent temperature conditioning, emergency diversion, pressure protection, acoustic-state control and a verified neutral transition in a novel way.

## 14. What cannot be claimed yet
Do not claim that THERMA owns thermoacoustic refrigeration, heat-driven refrigeration, reversible heat-pump operation, a regenerator, a resonator, helium pressurization, or heat-source recovery in the abstract.

## 15. Chat history evidence
The project conversation documents the chronology of concept development. It can support an internal invention record or laboratory notebook, but it is not itself a substitute for formal filing strategy or legal review.

## 16. Public-disclosure caution
Before making a public disclosure that could affect filing strategy, the project team should obtain professional patent advice appropriate to the jurisdictions of interest.

## 17. Patent-search workflow
1. Freeze the exact THERMA 3.5 architecture.
2. Identify every required subsystem and interaction.
3. Search scientific literature.
4. Search patent databases.
5. Build a claim chart.
6. Identify overlapping disclosures.
7. Separate old features from proposed combinations.
8. Draft alternative claim scopes.
9. Obtain professional legal review.

## 18. Scientific search baseline
The project dossier already cites representative literature including a comprehensive 2025 review of heat-driven thermoacoustic refrigeration, a 2026 study of a heat-driven refrigerator/heat pump, a 2025 reversible mode-switching study, and a 2023/2024 heat-driven refrigeration study.

## 19. Patent search baseline
The supplied dossier records prior-art examples including US5901556A, US20210404713A1/US11703255B2, US8584471B2, CN103808064A/B and CN108180673B. These records are starting points rather than an exhaustive search.

## 20. Search limitation
The project has not established an exhaustive global patent search. Therefore novelty conclusions must remain provisional.

## 21. Invention-disclosure structure
A formal disclosure should contain: title, inventors, problem, background, technical solution, system diagram, subsystem description, control method, safety method, alternatives, prototype evidence, dates, prior art, and signatures/records as required.

## 22. Inventor attribution
Inventor attribution should be determined by applicable law and the actual contribution to the claimed invention. A conversational project record does not automatically settle inventorship.

## 23. Version control
Each major THERMA architecture should be preserved with date, design assumptions, model version, diagrams, test state and decision rationale. This reduces accidental mixing of superseded concepts.

## 24. THERMA 3.5 architecture lock
The intended THERMA 3.5 core has fixed hardware with oscillating working gas. The core does not contain a piston, displacer, crankshaft, connecting rod, flywheel, mechanical compressor, or mechanical bearing power train.

## 25. Consequence for visual assets
Generated images that introduced piston/Stirling-style hardware are architecture-drift artifacts. They should not be used as authoritative THERMA 3.5 system drawings.

## 26. Visual evidence rule
The 35-plus generated images are communication assets. Their dimensions and embedded performance text are not authoritative unless separately supported by calculations or experiments.

## 27. Evidence labels
Recommended visual labels are: CONCEPTUAL, DESIGN STUDY, MODEL OUTPUT, EXPERIMENTAL, or VALIDATED. A generated render should normally remain CONCEPTUAL or DESIGN STUDY.

## 28. Novelty versus engineering value
A system can have high engineering value without a broad patent claim. Commercial differentiation may also arise from cost, reliability, serviceability, source-temperature flexibility, controls, manufacturing, or network integration.

## 29. Non-obviousness question
Even when individual pieces are known, a combination can require an inventive-step analysis. The correct question is whether the claimed combination would have been obvious to a skilled person in light of the identified prior art.

## 30. Claim drafting discipline
Claims should define structural and functional relationships precisely enough to distinguish prior art. Vague phrases such as “smart thermal system” are unlikely to be sufficient by themselves.

## 31. Functional versus structural language
Some claims may need structural relationships, while others may emphasize a control sequence or operating method. The final strategy should be determined by patent counsel.

## 32. Alternatives to preserve scope
The invention disclosure should record variations in working gas, resonator topology, regenerator matrix, heat-exchanger architecture, thermal-fluid medium, control method and module arrangement where supported by the concept.

## 33. Source-interface architecture
A key design choice is separating upstream energy generation from the THERMA pressure/acoustic core through a controlled secondary heat-transfer interface. This allows electricity, solar thermal, process heat, waste heat or other sources to be abstracted into the same thermal interface.

## 34. Extreme-input protection
A raw 1500 °C industrial stream should be intercepted upstream by a rated recovery exchanger or diverter rather than routed into the THERMA core. This safety architecture should be documented separately from any broad thermal-source claim.

## 35. Reversible architecture
The reversible mode should pass through a neutral/verified state. The control sequence can be documented as a possible process claim only after the exact implementation is defined and compared with prior art.

## 36. Distributed network concept
A factory-to-community thermal network could be a distinct system-level invention area if the precise network topology, control, isolation, load allocation and thermal-routing logic are novel and non-obvious.

## 37. Module arrangement
Parallel fixed thermoacoustic cores can improve part-load control and fault tolerance. A claim would require precise module interactions, not merely the statement “multiple modules.”

## 38. Serviceability
Replaceable regenerator and heat-exchanger cartridges may provide practical differentiation if the interface, qualification process and operating strategy are specific and novel.

## 39. Control and diagnostics
ATAPR is a project label for adaptive acoustic/thermal routing and control. It is not an established external standard or commercial product; any potential IP claim should describe the actual algorithms, states, signals and actuator relationships.

## 40. Prior-art mapping table
For each candidate feature, the project should record: feature ID, THERMA embodiment, closest prior-art document, overlapping disclosure, distinguishing feature, uncertainty, and legal question.

## 41. Example mapping categories
A feature can be classified as: clearly known, probably known, combination possibly new, implementation possibly new, or unresolved.

## 42. Search completeness
A serious search should cover English-language patents and literature plus jurisdiction-specific databases relevant to intended markets. Search terms should include both technical names and functional descriptions.

## 43. Inventive contribution record
The development journal should record which decisions were independently developed, which were prompted by literature, which emerged from simulation, and which were adopted from known engineering practice.

## 44. Simulation as evidence
Simulation can support technical reasoning and show internal consistency. It does not by itself prove novelty, patentability, manufacturability or market superiority.

## 45. Experimental evidence as evidence
A reproducible prototype dataset can support technical enablement and credibility. It still does not automatically make the concept patentable; prior art and claim scope remain separate questions.

## 46. Economic evidence
A cost advantage can be valuable commercially even if the underlying physical mechanism is well known. Economic claims should therefore be kept separate from novelty claims.

## 47. Environmental evidence
Waste-heat recovery may reduce purchased energy or emissions depending on the baseline. Environmental benefit should be calculated against a defined counterfactual, not asserted from the word “waste.”

## 48. Documentation retention
Keep the dated architecture, simulation assumptions, diagrams, laboratory records, supplier data, material certificates, test logs and decision notes together. The repository should preserve both successful and failed design paths.

## 49. Final IP status
Current status: no conclusion of patentability. THERMA has a documented concept, a development history, and candidate architectural differentiation, but requires formal search, exact claims and legal analysis.
