# 003 — Problem Statement

THERMA begins with a simple engineering opportunity: some industrial systems reject heat while nearby or co-located loads require cooling or useful heat. A thermal platform that can use a suitable temperature gradient for thermoacoustic power generation and then route acoustic work into refrigeration could connect these two streams.

The engineering challenge is not to prove that thermoacoustics exists. The challenge is to produce a compact, stable, serviceable, safe and economically useful system from known physical components.

The central questions are:

- How much acoustic power can a finite temperature gradient produce?
- How efficiently can that acoustic power be transmitted and converted into refrigeration?
- How much cooling can be delivered at a 0–3 °C cold target?
- How much useful heating can a reversible configuration provide from a low-temperature source?
- How strongly do heat-exchanger approach temperatures, regenerator losses, acoustic streaming and nonlinear effects reduce performance?
- What source temperature and sink conditions make the system practical?
- Can a fixed-hardware architecture remain maintainable through replaceable service modules?

The project history also exposed a second practical issue: a machine can be thermally efficient but still be inconvenient if it takes many hours to pull down a warm room or product load. That drove the 3.3 rapid-cooling concept, where steady thermal production and high short-duration cooling capacity are treated as separate design requirements.

The project therefore treats capacity, not just temperature, as a primary metric. A statement such as “3 °C output” only describes the cold-side temperature target. Cooling capacity must be stated in kW, and an operating schedule must show how that capacity is delivered over time.

For heating, the project used a conceptual 20 °C to 80 °C hot-water case. The energy required for one litre of water is approximately 69.8 Wh. The model must then determine how much low-grade source heat and work are required after accounting for the actual COP and temperature lift.

The waste-heat interface is equally important. Raw industrial streams must not enter the thermoacoustic core directly when their temperature or chemistry is outside the qualified envelope. A controlled secondary heat-transfer loop, recovery exchanger, diverter and safety layer must condition the source before it reaches the core.

The commercial problem is also explicit. THERMA competes with mature compressor refrigeration, absorption systems, adsorption systems and industrial heat pumps. It therefore needs a differentiated value proposition such as low-cost waste-heat utilization, modularity, source-temperature flexibility, low maintenance, reversible operation or distributed deployment.

The ultimate engineering problem can be stated as:

> Can GHIA build and validate a fixed-hardware thermoacoustic thermal platform whose measured cooling/heating performance, safety, reliability and economics justify its use in an application where waste heat or useful thermal gradients are available?

The answer cannot be established by simulation alone. The v1 dossier explicitly frames THERMA as a conceptual/pre-prototype project and identifies exact geometry, material life, pressure integrity, heat-exchanger performance, leakage and experimental validation as open items.
