# 016 — Economic Model and Commercial Benchmarks

## Purpose
Define the economic questions THERMA must answer against mature refrigeration and heat-pump technologies.

## 1. Economic boundary
A valid comparison must include capital equipment, source-energy preparation, auxiliaries, heat rejection, controls, maintenance, piping and installation.

## 2. Compressor benchmark
Compressor refrigeration is the primary reference for electrically powered cooling. It is mature, compact and widely serviced.

## 3. Absorption benchmark
Absorption cooling is an established heat-driven competitor. THERMA must show a specific advantage rather than assume waste heat use is sufficient.

## 4. Adsorption benchmark
Adsorption systems also use thermal driving input and can have low electrical consumption. Their cycle and power density should be used as comparison points.

## 5. Conventional heat pump benchmark
Electric heat pumps can deliver useful heating efficiently. THERMA heating mode must demonstrate value where a thermal input or low electrical demand creates an advantage.

## 6. Thermal-input cost
The economic value of THERMA strongly depends on the marginal cost of its driving heat.

## 7. Waste-heat case
Industrial waste heat may have low marginal cost because it would otherwise be rejected. Recovery hardware still has capital and maintenance costs.

## 8. Electric resistance case
Electricity-to-heat-to-THERMA refrigeration is usually economically inferior to direct electric compression. It is not the primary business case.

## 9. Gas/biomass case
Combustion can provide a suitable temperature but introduces fuel cost, emissions, burners and safety equipment. THERMA must compete with the alternatives.

## 10. Solar thermal case
Solar thermal can be attractive where solar availability and storage already exist. Source intermittency makes buffering important.

## 11. Engine waste heat
Engine exhaust and jacket-water heat can be attractive because the waste is produced with electrical or mechanical generation.

## 12. Steam and condensate
Industrial steam or condensate can provide stable thermal input. The source interface must handle pressure and temperature safely.

## 13. Geothermal/wastewater
Low-grade water can support heating mode or other thermal duties. The temperature lift and heat exchanger requirements determine feasibility.

## 14. Delivered cooling cost
A useful metric is delivered cooling cost per kWh of cooling, including source energy, auxiliaries, capital recovery and maintenance.

## 15. Delivered heating cost
For heating, compare delivered useful heat per kWh or per unit thermal input, again including all balance-of-plant costs.

## 16. Capital cost
Prototype cost is not commercial cost. Commercial equipment benefits from manufacturing scale, standardized modules and reduced test overhead.

## 17. Power density
A major commercial question is useful kW per machine volume and mass. Low power density can dominate cost even when energy is “free.”

## 18. Maintenance
No mechanical compressor does not mean zero maintenance. Pumps, fans, valves, sensors, seals, regenerators and heat exchangers remain service items.

## 19. Reliability premium
A customer may accept higher capital cost for a system that has high uptime or uses otherwise stranded heat. This must be demonstrated with maintenance and endurance data.

## 20. Part-load operation
A fixed-capacity system can waste thermal input at low demand. Modular cores can improve part-load operation if the cost of extra modules is justified.

## 21. Modular economics
Parallel modules can allow smaller units to operate while others are offline. This creates redundancy value but increases component count.

## 22. Thermal storage economics
Storage can shift waste heat from production time to demand time. Storage has thermal losses, tank cost, piping and controls.

## 23. Rapid cooling economics
Rapid mode may require additional modules or buffer capacity that sits underused for much of the day. The revenue or operational value of rapid pull-down must justify that capital.

## 24. Cold-room economics
A cold room has both pull-down and maintenance duty. The economic model must not size the machine for an extreme transient without evaluating utilization.

## 25. Restaurant economics
Restaurants may benefit if one thermal source supports both refrigeration and hot water. Simultaneous loads can improve utilization.

## 26. Dairy economics
Dairy facilities often have refrigeration and cleaning/process heat. Waste heat integration may reduce total energy expenditure.

## 27. Industrial economics
Industrial plants with constant waste heat are the strongest candidate because source availability is high and alternative thermal energy may be expensive.

## 28. Factory-to-town economics
A district network needs pipe capital, insulation, pumping, heat loss, land/route constraints and customer diversity. The source and users should be geographically close.

## 29. Payback
Payback = incremental investment / annual savings is a useful screening metric but should not replace NPV or lifecycle analysis.

## 30. Net present value
Commercial analysis should use discount rate, project life, residual value and replacement intervals.

## 31. Replacement model
A long-life thermal platform may still replace regenerators, valves, sensors, pumps, fans and electronics.

## 32. Financing
Debt, grant, strategic capital and equity have different cost of capital. Deep-tech prototypes may be more suitable for grants and milestone funding initially.

## 33. Prototype-to-pilot
Investors need measurable milestones: acoustic proof, thermal proof, endurance, customer pilot, cost model and certification path.

## 34. Grant logic
The first grant proposal should fund the uncertainty-reduction experiment rather than promise a fully commercial machine.

## 35. Investor risk
Prototype investors accept technical uncertainty. Misrepresentation is unacceptable; assumptions and unknowns should be explicit.

## 36. Competitive moat
Potential moat could come from system architecture, control, thermal routing, modular serviceability, integration know-how or validated performance.

## 37. Commodity risk
Heat exchangers, pumps and valves are commodity components. Any differentiation should be in the system-level architecture and measured performance.

## 38. Service network
A commercial product needs spare parts, inspection, calibration and trained service capability. “No compressor” is not equivalent to “no service network.”

## 39. Manufacturing scale
Cost could fall with standardized modules and repeatable pressure-vessel fabrication. This requires design-for-manufacture work.

## 40. Supply chain
Working gas, alloys, exchanger fabrication and instrumentation may create supply dependencies. Early prototypes should use commercially available qualified components where possible.

## 41. Environmental value
Avoided electricity or fuel consumption can create emissions savings. The calculation needs regional emission factors and complete auxiliary loads.

## 42. Heat-rejection value
If rejected heat is also useful, total site energy utilization can improve. This is a system-level benefit rather than a property of the acoustic core.

## 43. Customer proposition
A customer should buy THERMA because it reduces total thermal-service cost or solves a thermal integration problem better than alternatives.

## 44. Business-case evidence
The minimum business case requires measured cooling/heating capacity, measured input energy, duty cycle, capital estimate, maintenance burden and customer operating profile.

## 45. Benchmark table
The comparison should track efficiency metric, source-temperature flexibility, power density, moving parts, maintenance, noise, safety, cost and maturity for THERMA and competitors.

## 46. Current project conclusion
The strongest initial economic hypothesis remains high-utilization industrial waste heat. Small residential units are likely harder to justify until compactness and efficiency are proven.

## 47. Commercial risk
The largest economic risks are low power density, high exchanger cost, pressure-vessel cost, low low-grade-source performance and unexpected maintenance.

## 48. Mitigation
Prototype experiments should prioritize these economic uncertainties alongside physics. A technically impressive but expensive prototype is not automatically a viable product.

## 49. Decision gates
At each stage, ask: Is performance improving enough to justify capital? Is maintenance manageable? Does the customer have a low-cost thermal source? Does the system outperform an established alternative on a meaningful metric?

## 50. Closing principle
THERMA becomes commercial only when measured thermal service, total lifecycle economics and customer value are all better enough than alternatives for a defined application. Waste heat alone is not a business model.
